Fourteen questions, one network
1Directed weighted graph

What exactly is the network?

Each docking station is a node. Each hire from dock i to dock j adds one unit of weight to the directed link i → j. Hires that return to the dock they left ( of the total) count towards a station's activity but stay out of the topology.

A third of all possible links exist, and any two stations are at most three hops apart. At this density the yes-or-no graph says very little. The information is in the weights.

2Heavy tails, maximum likelihood, Lorenz curves

How unequal is the traffic?

Less than you might expect for the stations. A station's strength is its journeys in plus out. Fitting its upper tail by maximum likelihood (Clauset, Shalizi and Newman's method) gives a power-law exponent of , but only for the top stations, and a log-normal describes the same tail just as well (Vuong's test, ). There are busy stations, but no runaway hubs. A dock has a fixed number of slots, and that limits how big any one station can grow.

The links are a different story. of the station pairs in use saw a single journey all month, while of the links carry half of all trips. The link Gini coefficient is , against for stations. Link weights are log-normal, and a pure power law is rejected ().

Strength grows faster than the number of partners: s ∝ k (R² = ). A station with more partners also sends more trips down each one (Barrat et al., 2004), so busy stations are busy because of heavy links as well as many links.

3Disparity filter (Serrano, Boguñá and Vespignani, 2009)

Which links actually matter?

Keeping only the heaviest links would erase the outer boroughs, whose links are all small. The disparity filter judges each link locally instead. It asks whether the link takes a larger share of its station's traffic than a random split would give, and keeps it if the probability α of that happening by chance is small, at either end.

At α = 0.001 the filter keeps links and stations. A weight threshold keeping the same number of links drops to stations.

4Spatial networks, gravity and radiation models

How far will people ride, and what bends the rule?

Half of all classic-bike hires end less than from where they started, in a straight line. For e-bikes the median is .

How much of the network does distance explain on its own? A doubly constrained gravity model fixes every station's real departures and arrivals, using iterative proportional fitting, and leaves one free function: how trips decay with distance. Fitted by Poisson maximum likelihood, an exponential decay of per km reproduces of all flows, measured by the common part of commuters. Trips halve every km. A power law fits much worse. The radiation model, built for commuting between cities, fails at this scale ().

The Thames is a barrier. Crossing it costs the same as riding an extra .

Adding one parameter for whether the straight line between two docks crosses the river improves the model by thousands of log-likelihood units. Below 1 km, pairs on opposite banks get of the trips that distance alone predicts, while same-bank pairs get . Even in central London, bridges are scarce and the river is wide.

    5PageRank, HITS, eigenvector, betweenness on effective distance

    Which stations are central, and in what sense?

    "Central" means different things. PageRank is where a random rider who keeps hiring ends up. Hubs and authorities (HITS) separate stations that send trips to important destinations from the destinations themselves. Betweenness and closeness need a notion of distance. Here they use Brockmann and Helbing's effective distance, 1 − ln Pij, where Pij is the share of station i's riders who go to j. A link is short if many riders use it.

      6Leiden, Infomap, null models, Laplacian spectrum, functional roles

      Does London split into regions of its own?

      Yes, and two unrelated methods agree on them. Leiden maximises modularity, meaning more trips inside groups than a network with the same station strengths would give. At resolution γ = 1 it finds regions (Q = , stable across 24 random seeds, mean NMI ). Random networks with the same expected strengths reach Q = , so the real value sits about standard deviations above chance.

      Infomap works from a different principle: it finds the regions where a random cyclist following the directed flows stays trapped, by compressing the description of its path. It also returns modules, matching Leiden's (NMI ).

      Resolution

      Who holds the regions together

      Guimerà and Amaral's cartography places each station by two numbers: how dominant it is inside its own region (z) and how evenly its trips spread across regions (participation P). Most stations are peripheral. A few are connector hubs: busy, and spread across regions. These are King's Cross, Waterloo, Soho, Old Street, London Bridge and Liverpool Street, the interchanges that stitch the city together. Only are provincial hubs that dominate their own region without bridging.

      7Rich club, assortativity, clustering, triad motifs

      What patterns repeat beyond single links?

      The busiest stations form a rich club. Opsahl's weighted coefficient compares the trips the top stations exchange among themselves with the same links carrying randomly reshuffled weights. The ten busiest stations exchange × more than chance. The effect fades as the club grows.

      Mixing, on the other hand, is neutral. Strength assortativity is and degree assortativity . Busy and quiet stations trade with each other in proportion to their size.

      Triangles that go both ways

      On the backbone (α = 0.01), every three-station pattern was counted and compared with 40 copies rewired at random with the same in- and out-degrees (Milo et al., 2002). Fully reciprocal triangles are the most over-represented pattern by far: observed against expected. Open one-way chains and cycles are rare.

      The backbone is made of small neighbourhoods where people ride back the way they came, not of one-way loops.

      8Temporal networks: hourly layers, regimes, time-resolved centrality

      When does the network change shape?

      Twice every working day, with a single slow wave at weekends. The working days and weekend and bank-holiday days are averaged separately, so every number is per day. Use the clock on the map to scrub through the day. The label follows the station with the highest PageRank in that hour.

      The centre of the network moves. It sits at St James's Square and Bank in the morning, Exhibition Road (the museums and Hyde Park) from late morning, Waterloo in the evening peak, and Battersea and the East End at night.

      Each hour of each day type is a network layer, 48 in all. Comparing every pair of layers by cosine similarity of their link weights reveals five regimes: night, dawn, morning commute, daytime and late evening. The evening peak looks like daytime, not like the morning, because it is the morning in reverse.

      The evening undoes the morning

      Stations that fill up between 7 and 10 am empty out between 4 and 7 pm, and vice versa. Across all stations, the morning and evening net balances correlate at r = . The links themselves reverse too. Take the 300 busiest morning links. The typical one carries trips in May's morning peaks and gets back in the evening, but only in the same direction.

      10Markov chains, spectral gap, validation against an independent dataset

      Where would the bikes pile up?

      Treat a bike as a random walker. Its next dock depends only on where it stands, with probabilities given by the trips out of that dock. The second eigenvalue of this Markov chain is , so a bike forgets where it started after about rides. A bike in use makes about rides a day, so it is anywhere within a day or two.

      Left alone, the walk drains some docks and floods others. The map colours each station by its net daily balance. Blue stations gain bikes, led by Bank, St James's Square and Liverpool Street. Amber stations lose them, led by Waterloo, Marylebone and the edges of Hyde Park.

      Who moves the bikes back?

      Every hire record carries a bike number, so every bike's month can be followed. times a bike was hired again after a journey. In of those (), the next hire started at a different dock from where the bike had been left. No customer rode it there. These are the operator's van and staff moves, plus the odd repair or data gap.       12Targeted attacks, percolation, Granovetter's weak ties

      What would break it?

      Close stations one by one and count what survives: journeys whose two docks are still open, and the largest connected piece of the strict backbone (α = 0.001). Nobody reroutes in this model, so it measures how concentrated today's demand is. It is not a forecast of behaviour.

      Strong links sit inside, weak links bridge

      Granovetter argued that weak social ties bridge communities. Onnela et al. (2007) tested this on phone calls. The same test works here. The overlap of two stations' neighbourhoods rises with the weight of the link between them, from to . Removing links from the weakest up breaks the backbone sooner than removing them from the strongest down.

      14Embeddings: t-SNE, force-directed layout, spectral coordinates

      London, as the bikes see it

      Throw away the street map and place each station by where its riders go and come from. The flow map runs t-SNE on each station's Hellinger-transformed origin and destination profile. The force layout draws the significant links as springs. The spectral layout uses the first non-trivial eigenvectors of the normalised Laplacian. Each is rotated and scaled onto geography (Procrustes), so stations glide from one view to the next.

      The flow map keeps each station's flow neighbours (trustworthiness ) and is still recognisably London: flow distances and geographic distances correlate at . Cycling is local. The stations that move furthest are the edges of the network (the Isle of Dogs, Canada Water, Putney, Stratford), which are pulled into the orbit of the hubs their riders actually use.

        Cleaning

        Hires were deduplicated by rental id and limited to positive durations within May. Stations were matched to current BikePoint docks by normalised name. hires () matched. The rest touch six retired or renamed docks and are excluded. Bank holidays (4 and 25 May) count as weekend days.

        What this cannot say

        Lines join docks. They are not routes ridden. A hire is not a person: one commuter can account for many links. One month cannot separate season from habit. Relocations are inferred from bike ids and include repairs and data gaps. Closure curves assume nobody reroutes. Occupancy comes from a different month than the journeys, which makes the validation conservative, not exact.
